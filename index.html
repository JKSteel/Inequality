Why the rich keep getting richer

Picture a population of households, each starting with a different amount of wealth and sharing one economy. Every household has its own run of good and bad luck over time. Now replay that exact population twice under near-identical conditions — the same households with the same starting wealth and the same run of luck — changing one thing only: how much wealth is redistributed each year. Model assumptions

What this is. The Bouchaud-Mézard model [1] is a physics-of-economics (econophysics) model about what happens to wealth when it compounds and is subject to volatility (i.e. luck). Both panels follow the same households in the same economy, with each household dealt the same run of luck in both. The only difference between them is J: how much wealth gets redistributed each year.

  • Mechanism. Wealth compounds equally for every household, but each year's growth carries a random element from volatility — some good years, some bad. Because that growth applies to what you already own, a bigger pile means a bigger swing, and lucky streaks snowball.
  • Result. Leave the system alone and wealth inequality increases; a smaller and smaller group ends up with almost everything. Redistribute as much as the mid-century did and it holds at a far healthier spread instead. That era sat right on the critical line J = σ2/2 (the boundary below which the spread of wealth keeps growing) so even "strong" redistribution here is only just enough to hold the line, not comfortably past it.
  • Takeaway. Redistribution of wealth doesn't shrink the economy, as historical data has shown [the model takes this as given: both economies grow at the same rate, m]. Both systems grow overall, and the low-redistribution side grows no faster than the high-redistribution side (if anything it's usually a bit slower). What changes is who gets the growth. The value of the whole economy pulls away from the typical household: the pie grows, the median gets left behind [2]. This is because most wealth is funnelled to the top.
  • One run is one sample. When wealth is this concentrated, the exact numbers depend on the luck of a handful of households. The "usually" ranges under each figure show how much they move across other random samples; press New sample to see it.
Key (what the variables mean and where the numbers come from)
m, economic growth
Wealth growth rate per year. For developed countries, 2%/yr is roughly the long-run growth in real GDP per person. Both panels use the same m; redistribution rate doesn't affect growth rate, which is well supported by the historical record [3], [4], [5] [though not unanimously: some studies find short-run gains in output from cutting top tax rates [6]]. If anything, giving both panels the same growth rate is conservative: the IMF finds that lower inequality (after taxes and transfers) is robustly correlated with faster and more durable growth, while redistribution itself is generally benign for growth [5], [2].
σ, volatility (luck)
How much the returns vary from year to year. This is the part of a household's outcome that's luck, not skill. Calvet, Campbell, & Sodini [7] tracked every Swedish household's portfolio and found a median volatility of 19.5%. Intelligence and hard work can lift your average return a little, but luck dominates: even among professional wealth advisers, Kahneman found essentially no correlation between one year's performance ranking and the next [8].
J, redistribution
How much wealth is moved each year from those above average wealth to those below it, through taxes, transfers, public spending, and the like. Note that J is not itself a tax rate.

In practice, J is a dependent variable. The model's long-run shape follows a Pareto distribution with exponent μ = 1 + 2J/σ2, where μ and σ are empirically measured (lower μ = more extreme concentration).a

Measuring μ. For a Pareto tail, the top 0.01%'s share of wealth divided by the top 0.1%'s share equals 0.11−1/μ, so the US wealth shares in Saez & Zucman [9] give μ directly. In the "golden age" it was around 2.0 (2.2% / 7% in 1978); in 2012 it was approximately 1.4 (11.2% / 22.0% in 2012). Today it is worse still: on the Federal Reserve's measure, the top 0.1%'s share of US wealth rose from 11.7% in 2012 to 15.0% in 2026 [10] [the Fed's survey-based figures understate the very top, so they show the trend rather than a directly comparable μ]. Independent estimates agree: US tax records put the Pareto exponent of capital income at a plateau of 2.0 in 1960–70 [11], and surveys and rich lists put today's wealth tail at about 1.5 [12]. Direct wealth estimates show the same shape over time: the top 0.1% held about 10% of US wealth through the 1950s and 60s and 7% in 1978, against 22% in 2012 [9]. Over the same period, cuts to US top tax rates were associated with rising concentration of income at the top [3]. These values of μ imply J values of approximately 0.8% today and 1.9% in the mid-century.b

There is a critical J value above which the spread of wealth settles and below which it keeps growing — the rich keep getting richer relative to everyone else. This critical point (μ = 2) depends solely on the volatility, which with the empirical value above gives Jc = σ2/2 = 1.9%. Today, we sit below this point; the mid-century was right at the threshold. The spread of wealth readout under each panel tracks this: it is the standard deviation of wealth divided by the average.c

"Typical" means the median household, not the average/mean, which the very rich skew upward.

Why the two economy sizes (pies) differ. Redistribution shuffles wealth around, it doesn't make or destroy any by itself, so averaged over many runs the two pies are exactly the same size. In any single run, though, they drift apart because growth is multiplicative: once a few hold most of the wealth, the whole economy's growth rides on them, including their luck. That makes the concentrated pie more volatile, and a more volatile pie usually ends up a little smaller (and occasionally much bigger), so in most runs it finishes behind the more equal one. [This is a small-sample effect: it shrinks as the number of households grows, and across a real country's millions of households it is negligible.]

Real-world approximation. Adds wealth-dependent returns: richer households earn higher returns of approximately 2.8 percentage points more per year per order of magnitude of wealth [13] [derived from the returns by wealth bracket reported there, not stated directly]. This is modelled as a pure transfer: total wealth is conserved each step, so what the wealthy gain in dollar terms comes directly out of everyone else's pockets, not from new growth.d It is motivated by Rognlie (2016) [14] (the entire rise in the net capital share since 1948 comes from housing, driven by scarcity rather than new investment) and Stiglitz (2012) [15] (excess returns reflect rent-seeking, not wealth creation). With this on, the μ formula and era labels above no longer apply.e It still gives every household the same volatility.f

If simulating the model beyond a normal lifetime, read each household as a family line and the wealth as inherited, with a new generation roughly every 25 years (the "gen" count beside the year).

  1. The original paper [1] writes this as 1 + J/σ2 because it defines the noise variance as 2σ2; here σ is ordinary volatility.
  2. The model takes roughly 1/J ≈ 50 years to forget where it started, so the mid-century distribution may still have partly reflected the large fortunes destroyed by the Depression and two world wars, not just that era's redistribution.
  3. Below the threshold the spread climbs erratically, because it is driven by a handful of the very richest pulling away. The top 10% and top 1% shares level off on both sides of the threshold, just at a higher level the lower J is. The model is also slow to settle, roughly 1/J years, so most of a 100-year run is still the journey from the fairly equal starting point.
  4. This choice doesn't change the top shares (they come out the same if the extra return is new growth), but it does push the typical household and the whole economy lower, so read those two numbers as the pessimistic case.
  5. With this on, a household that gets far enough ahead earns enough extra to outrun redistribution and pulls away from everyone else. Whether and when that happens depends on luck, the number of households and how long you run it, so there is no fixed tipping point. The 2.8 points is also extended beyond the wealth range it was measured on, which exaggerates the effect for the very richest.
  6. In reality, volatility is not the same for everyone, and the difference hurts the bottom half. Their wealth is mostly a thin slice of equity in a mortgaged home, while the richest mostly own shares and businesses [16], so a modest fall in house prices wipes out much of it. In the 2008 crash the bottom 50%'s net worth fell by over 80% and took until 2018 to recover, while the top 1%'s fell 21% and recovered by 2012 [10].
